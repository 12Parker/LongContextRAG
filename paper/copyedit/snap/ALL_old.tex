\begin{abstract}
Retrieval-augmented generation systems increasingly detect contradictions among retrieved passages and flag them to
the generator. We ask what such a flag must say, and how accurate its detector must be, for it to help against
misinformation. On fixed passages from five benchmarks, with seven generators from three model families, we vary the
detector and the format of its flag, in hypotheses pre-registered in stages (H1--H9 before any confirmatory data,
H10--H31 in eight later addenda). A perfectly accurate neutral flag (``these passages conflict'') does not reliably
help: what it does depends on the answer policy and the model family, and pooled over \neuMlK\ settings, allowing
for shared questions, its effect is \neuMlMu\ points \neuMlCI. Naming the false side helps, but on average no more than
stating the wrong answer outright (pooled difference \PoolTwentyThree\ points, equivalent within $\pm$3 on average). In a
randomised test, a flag naming the false side
raises accuracy by \hThirtyOneAMin\ to \hThirtyOneAMax\ points and one naming the correct side lowers it by
\ahThirtyOneBMax\ to \ahThirtyOneBMin, so randomised flags on true pairs help only above a break-even direction
precision that rises with the generator's accuracy, from \beMin\ to \beMax. Real detectors are selective, so this
threshold alone can misjudge them; combined with each cell's coverage and no-flag accuracy, the follow rates from this
test account retrospectively for the flag's effect in \predEightN\ crossed detector--generator cells
($r=\predEightR$, mean error \predEightMae\ points; exploratory). What a
detector contributes is its verdict: passed as an answer key it matches the flag within 3 points. On genuine
conflicts direction removes valid answers, and when conflicts are rare a real detector's false flags on clean
questions can outweigh its gains. We release the harness, the pre-registrations and every run.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) \citep{lewis2020rag} hands a language model passages that may disagree. Some
disagreements are genuine; others are planted or stale misinformation \citep{zou2025poisonedrag,wang2025madam}. A natural fix is to detect the contradiction and tell
the generator. Recent systems pair contradiction detectors with prompts that flag conflicting passages
\citep{wang2026conflictrag}, and prompting a model about conflicts can help \citep{cattan2025conflicts}. These systems
report detection F1 and end-to-end accuracy, but not how the two connect. What should the flag say, and what must the
detector get right for it to help?

We fix the retrieved passages and vary, independently, the \emph{detector} (heuristic, NLI, LLM, random placebo,
oracle, and synthetic detectors of chosen quality) and the \emph{format} that passes its output to the generator: a
neutral ``these passages may conflict'' flag, a flag that says one side is false without saying which, a
\emph{directional} flag that names the likely misinformation, or removal of the suspect passage. After pilots on
RAMDocs \citep{wang2025madam}, we pre-registered our hypotheses (H1--H9) and tested them on 300 held-out questions;
eight addenda (H10--H31), each committed before its runs, extended the study to ConflictBank \citep{su2024conflictbank}
(including a variant where the misinformation outnumbers the truth), to RAGuard \citep{zeng2025raguard}, whose
misleading side is real Reddit posts, and to seven generators from OpenAI, Anthropic and Qwen. Controls separate what a
flag conveys: forced choice, an answer key, randomised direction, the detector's verdict and its model's own answer as
hints, and six detectors crossed with six generators. WikiContradict \citep{hou2024wikicontradict}, whose conflicts are genuine, is the contrast case.

Our findings:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{A correct neutral flag does not reliably help.} Asked for every valid answer, OpenAI and Qwen models add the
misinformation to their answer; asked for one answer, some generators abstain on misinformed and correct answers
alike; under forced choice the effect is small. Claude models move less, and the minimal prompt is the most harmful
policy. These policy and family splits were coded after seeing data.
\item \textbf{An oracle's direction is a ceiling.} Naming the false side helps on every benchmark that labels one, but
on average it is equivalent to an answer key that names the wrong answer (pooled over \PoolTwentyThreeK\
comparisons, which vary individually).
\item \textbf{A directional flag pays only above a break-even precision.} When direction is randomised (H31), a wrong
flag costs more than a right one gains, because generators follow both at similar rates and an accurate generator has
more correct answers to lose. The break-even direction precision rises from \beMin\ for gpt-4o-mini to \beMax\ for
gpt-6.1-terra. It calibrates flags on true pairs; for real detectors, the follow rates combined with what each
flags account for the effects (retrospective; Figure~\ref{fig:reliability}), and precision alone can mislead. The detector's verdict as an answer key matches its flag (H30); the detector model's
own answer as a hint does worse (H27).
\item \textbf{Direction is unsafe on genuine conflicts and costly when conflicts are rare.} On WikiContradict the LLM
directional flag and removal cut the share of answers giving both valid sides (H28), and at a 5\% share of conflict
questions the real detector's flag lowers accuracy in \prevNegFive\ of \prevN\ settings.
\end{itemize}
On CONFLICTS, the one benchmark of real search results, no condition changes accuracy significantly. We release the harness, pre-registrations and every run.\footnote{Code, the pre-registration and
all run outputs are in the anonymised supplementary material and will be released publicly on acceptance.}
\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Knowledge conflicts.}
Language models can prefer memorised knowledge over contradicting context \citep{longpre2021entity}, yet are readily
persuaded by coherent contradicting evidence \citep{xie2024chameleon}, to a degree that depends on the strength of
their prior \citep{wu2024clasheval}; \citet{huang2025trust} train models to decide which of context and memory to
trust, including on real incorrect Reddit contexts, and context-faithful prompting changes how readily models follow
or abstain on conflicting context \citep{zhou2023context}. Of the conflict types of \citet{xu2024survey}, we study inter-context
conflicts, as in \citet{chen2022rich}, who find that models tend to rely on a single passage when evidence conflicts,
and \citet{wan2024convincing}, who find that models weigh a passage's relevance more than its credibility cues.

\paragraph{Benchmarks.}
RAMDocs \citep{wang2025madam}, built on AmbigDocs \citep{lee2024ambigdocs}, mixes genuine ambiguity, misinformation
and noise. CONFLICTS \citep{cattan2025conflicts} annotates search results with conflict types, each with its own
desired behaviour, and WikiContradict \citep{hou2024wikicontradict} pairs genuinely contradicting Wikipedia passages.
FaithEval \citep{ming2025faitheval}, ConflictBank \citep{su2024conflictbank}, ConfRAG \citep{yuan2026confrag},
QACC \citep{liu2025qacc} and WhoQA \citep{pham2024whoqa} cover related settings. RAMDocs and ConflictBank label which side is misinformation; WikiContradict does not.

\paragraph{Telling the model about conflicts.}
Prompting a model to reason about the conflict type helps, and supplying the gold type helps much more
\citep{cattan2025conflicts}; on WikiContradict, models acknowledge both sides more often when told the passages may
conflict \citep{hou2024wikicontradict}. Ragability \citep{gross2026ragability} finds that such a hint helps on
contradictory contexts but hurts on \emph{consistent} ones, where it is false. In our neutral-flag result the hint is
true and the input contradictory, and accuracy still falls, because the hint does not say which side to trust.
\citet{pan2023risk} test detection and ``vigilant'' prompting against LLM-generated misinformation, and
\citet{pan2023attacking} call for misinformation-aware QA. Source metadata and credibility signals also shape how models resolve conflicts
\citep{chiang2024metadata,ge2025confact}.

\paragraph{Telling the model which passage is wrong.}
Closest to our directional format, \citet{hong2024gullible} pass a discriminator's marks on perturbed passages to the
generator in the prompt, which makes it more robust to counterfactual noise. CrAM \citep{deng2025cram} rescales
attention by passage credibility, RA-RAG \citep{hwang2025rarag} estimates source reliability, and
\citet{weller2024defending} defend open-domain QA against disinformation. TACS \citep{yu2024tacs} detects untruthful context and masks it,
close to our removal format. FaithfulRAG \citep{zhang2025faithfulrag} models
conflicts at the level of facts before generating. These works, with \citet{pan2023risk}'s finding that vigilant
prompts give limited protection, already show that a reliability signal can help more than a warning, and some vary
the signal's quality \citep{hong2024gullible,pan2024credibility}. What we add is a randomised manipulation of
direction on the same pairs, answer-key controls that separate the verdict from the marking, its cost on
conflict-free questions, and a break-even account of when a real detector's verdict helps a given generator. RGB \citep{chen2024rgb} warns models that passages may contain errors, and they still rarely
detect them; our neutral flag is the pair-specific version of that warning. Models also follow injected hints and asserted views, wrong
ones included \citep{turpin2023unfaithful,sharma2024sycophancy}, and advice improves a decision-maker only when its
errors fall where the decision-maker is right less often \citep{bansal2021accurate,mozannar2020defer}, which is the
condition our break-even makes quantitative. Whether
abstaining is a failure depends on the cost of a wrong answer \citep{wen2025abstention,song2025trustscore}.

\paragraph{Detection, resolution and poisoning.}
ConflictRAG \citep{wang2026conflictrag} trains a pairwise detector with an LLM fallback and reports pair-level F1
alongside answer gains; its NLI and chain-of-thought baselines resemble two of our detectors. LLMs detect conflicts but struggle to pinpoint them
\citep{gokul2025contradiction,wang2024resolving,jiayang2024econ}, favour the more frequent side, the one matching
their prior and LLM-generated passages \citep{jin2024tugofwar,kortukov2024realistic,tan2024blinded}, and are
distracted by irrelevant context \citep{shi2023distracted,schuster2026whose}. TCR \citep{ye2026tcr}, context-aware
decoding \citep{shi2024cad}, EvidentialRAG \citep{hossain2026evidentialrag} and credibility-aware generation
\citep{pan2024credibility} learn or decode around unreliable passages, and MADAM-RAG \citep{wang2025madam}, Astute RAG
\citep{wang2025astute}, Self-RAG \citep{asai2024selfrag}, Corrective RAG \citep{yan2024crag} and InstructRAG
\citep{wei2025instructrag} handle them without an explicit contradiction detector. A few injected passages can steer a RAG
system \citep{zou2025poisonedrag}; RobustRAG \citep{xiang2024robustrag} isolates passages for certifiable robustness,
and TrustRAG \citep{zhou2025trustrag} filters suspected poisoned ones, the analogue of our removal. We ask instead how
a detector's output should be passed to the generator.
\section{Framework}
\label{sec:method}

A question $q$ comes with retrieved passages $P=(p_0,\dots,p_{n-1})$. A \emph{detector} returns flagged pairs
$F\subseteq\{(i,j): i<j\}$; a \emph{directional} detector also returns, for each flagged pair, a \emph{suspect}
set $S_{ij}\subseteq\{i,j\}$: the side it believes is misinformation. A \emph{format} turns $(P,F,S)$ into the
generator's prompt. We vary detectors and formats independently. Every prompt shares a skeleton: passages, question,
and an instruction to end with a \texttt{Final answer(s):} line. The \emph{default} skeleton asks for every valid
answer when the passages support several (RAMDocs contains same-name ambiguity, so a correct answer may need several
entities). The \emph{minimal} skeleton drops that policy, and its answer line allows several answers without inviting
them. The \emph{single} skeleton says the question has exactly one correct answer (true of ConflictBank by
construction). All three allow ``unknown'' when no passage answers the question; the \emph{forced} skeleton is
\emph{single} without that option (Appendix~\ref{app:prompts}).

\subsection{Formats}
\label{sec:formats}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item[No flags (plain).] Passages and question only.
\item[Instruction.] Plain plus a generic instruction to check the passages for contradictions, with no detector.
v1 ends ``if a disagreement reflects genuinely different valid answers, report each one''; v2 asks the model to
judge which passages are reliable and to leave out answers supported only by unreliable ones.
\item[Flag.] Each flagged passage gets the header \texttt{[POTENTIAL CONFLICT with passage(s): j]}, followed by a list
\texttt{Passages i and j: possible contradiction}. This is the original CAAG format, except that every detector's
flags are rendered identically, so detectors differ only in \emph{which} pairs they flag.
\item[One-wrong flag.] As Flag, but adds that exactly one passage of the pair contains misinformation, without saying
which: it says that the conflict is misinformation, but not its direction.
\item[Directional flag.] As Flag, but suspect passages are headed \texttt{[LIKELY MISINFORMATION; conflicts with
passage(s): j]}, and the list says which passage likely contains misinformation.
\item[Controls.] \emph{Pick}: the neutral flag plus ``decide which side is better supported and answer with that
side only''. \emph{No pairs}: each flag without its pair structure (flagged passages are marked, but not which conflicts
with which). \emph{Answer key}: plain plus ``The answer $X$ is false'' for each wrong answer, with no passage marked.
\item[Remove (suspect).] The suspect passages of every flagged pair are deleted. For a pair without a suspect, the
second passage is deleted, as the original CAAG \textsc{remove} strategy does without retrieval scores.
\item[Single call.] No detector: one call that first writes \texttt{Suspect passages: \dots} and then answers from
the other passages, at half the calls of a detector pipeline.
\item[Closed book.] No passages; it measures what the generator knows without retrieval.
\end{description}

\subsection{Detectors}
\label{sec:detectors}
\begin{description}[leftmargin=0pt,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item[CAAG] (Contradiction-Aware Attention Gating) is a heuristic detector from an earlier, unpublished RAG system,
used with its code unchanged. It scores similar pairs by negation, entity-mismatch and temporal cues, runs locally
and gives no direction (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}).
\item[NLI] is \texttt{nli-deberta-v3-base} \citep{he2023debertav3}, a cross-encoder scored in both directions; a pair is
flagged if the larger contradiction probability exceeds $0.5$. It gives no direction.
\item[LLM] is a single call that lists contradicting pairs, telling them apart from ambiguity, complementary and
temporal relations, and names the side more likely to be misinformation (or ``both'' or ``unclear''), as JSON. It is
gpt-4o-mini (temperature 0) unless stated; in the crossed design gpt-4.1, gpt-6-luna, gpt-6.1-terra, Claude Haiku 5.5
and Claude Sonnet 5.5 run the same prompt.
\item[Random] flags each pair independently at CAAG's per-pair flag rate, a placebo that matches CAAG's flag volume.
\item[Oracle] flags exactly the gold contradiction pairs and names the gold misinformation side. The
\emph{inverted oracle}, an adversarial control, flags the same pairs but names the correct side.
\end{description}

\subsection{Synthetic detectors}
\label{sec:synthetic}
To separate a detector's accuracy from its identity, we build detectors from the gold labels with a chosen pair-level
precision $p\in\{0.25,0.5,0.75,1\}$, recall $r\in\{0.25,0.5,1\}$ and direction accuracy $d\in\{0.5,0.75,1\}$ (36
cells), each rendered as a directional flag and as removal. All cells reuse the same random draws, so they are nested
and comparisons across cells are paired (Appendix~\ref{app:grid}).
\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Data.}
\textbf{RAMDocs} \citep{wang2025madam} has 500 questions, each with 1--12 documents (5.5 on average) typed
\emph{correct} (supports a gold answer, possibly for a different same-name entity), \emph{misinfo} (supports a wrong
answer) or \emph{noise}. A pair is a \emph{contradiction} if one side is misinformation and the answers differ,
\emph{ambiguity} if both are correct with different answers, \emph{consistent} if the answers match, and
\emph{unrelated} if either is noise. A question is a \emph{conflict} question if it has a contradiction pair, and
\emph{clean} otherwise. Some documents typed \emph{correct} contain a wrong answer; questions whose only sign of
conflict is such a document count only in ``All'' (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\textbf{ConflictBank} \citep{su2024conflictbank}: for a Wikidata fact, an LLM writes one evidence passage for the true
object and one for a substituted object. We sample 400 questions round-robin over relations: 300 conflict questions
(correct passage, misinformation passage, two distractors from other subjects) and 100 clean ones (correct passage,
three distractors). Every question has one gold answer. It had no pilot; its registered tests are addenda (Table~\ref{tab:claims}), and everything else on it is exploratory. A
\emph{majority} variant gives each conflict question the correct passage and ConflictBank's three wrong-object
passages for that fact (its misinformation, temporal and semantic conflicts, all supporting the substituted object,
which we count as misinformation), so the false side outnumbers the truth three to one.

\textbf{RAGuard} \citep{zeng2025raguard} pairs PolitiFact claims (true or false) with Reddit posts that an LLM
labelled supporting, misleading or unrelated, so the misleading side is real online text. We keep 294 claims with at
least one supporting and one misleading post (up to two of each, plus two unrelated posts) and 100 claims with no
misleading post; the question is whether the claim is true. Its post labels are behavioural: a post is supporting or misleading according to whether GPT-4 judged the claim correctly with it in context \citep[\S3.3]{zeng2025raguard}. They do not verify that two posts contradict or that the misleading one is false, so on RAGuard the ``oracle'' transfers these labels and is not a factual oracle (audit in Appendix~\ref{app:semantic}).

\textbf{WikiContradict} \citep{hou2024wikicontradict} has 253 questions, each with two genuinely contradicting
Wikipedia passages; the reference answer is \emph{both} sides. We score whether an answer gives both; the original evaluation also asks that it acknowledge the incompatibility and not prefer a side without support \citep[\S3.4]{hou2024wikicontradict}, so our WikiContradict measure is coverage of the competing sources (the original rubric is in Appendix~\ref{app:semantic}). We also run 237 automatically scorable
\textbf{CONFLICTS} \citep{cattan2025conflicts} questions (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}).

\paragraph{Pre-registration and splits.}
We developed the method on a random 200-question pilot split of RAMDocs. Before running anything on the remaining 300
questions (139 conflict, 126 clean, 35 excluded), we committed a pre-registration that fixes the conditions, metrics,
hypotheses and corrections. Every RAMDocs result in the main text is on this confirmatory split; pilot results appear
only in the appendix. Comparisons that were not registered, including every real-detector row of
Table~\ref{tab:main}, are exploratory. Eight addenda registered H10--H31, each committed before its own runs, so
these hypotheses were formed after the original confirmatory results were known; the confirmatory core registered
before any confirmatory data is H1--H9. The last two addenda also registered re-analyses of data we had already seen, and a final
round of re-analyses (pooled answer-key comparisons, break-even precision, the prediction of the crossed cells,
prevalence reweighting and a programme-wide FDR) was not registered at all. A ninth addendum (\S17) registered further re-analyses, corrected baselines and a semantic re-score, after a mock review and before their runs. All of these are exploratory
(Table~\ref{tab:claims}). The repository's commit history is the only timestamp. Appendix~\ref{app:repro} lists every deviation.

\paragraph{Generators and baselines.}
The main generator is \texttt{gpt-4o-mini} \citep{openai2024gpt4o} (temperature 0.1, at most 256 output tokens, seeds
0--2). We replicate key conditions with \texttt{gpt-4.1}, Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct \citep{yang2024qwen25} (4-bit, local),
two current reasoning models, \texttt{gpt-6-luna} (3 seeds) and \texttt{gpt-6.1-terra} (seed 0; one sample), and Anthropic's
Claude Haiku 5.5 (3 seeds) and Claude Sonnet 5.5 (seed 0). The reasoning and Claude models run at their default
temperature with up to 4,096 output tokens, since they reason before answering. The LLM detector is gpt-4o-mini
except in the crossed design (H24), where six detectors and six generators form \hTwentyFourCells\ pairs on ConflictBank. On ConflictBank the primary prompt is the single skeleton; the default and
minimal skeletons are robustness checks. We rerun the oracle and LLM-detector conditions with the minimal skeleton.
MADAM-RAG \citep{wang2025madam} is ported unchanged (three debate rounds). Astute RAG \citep{wang2025astute} and
TrustRAG \citep{zhou2025trustrag} use their published prompts and defaults; since both return one answer, we also run
\emph{multi} variants that ask for every valid answer (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}). We also run the LLM detector on
Qwen2.5-7B with gpt-4o-mini generating. A detector pipeline counts as one detector call plus one generator call. Two
controls test whether a crossed cell's gain is only a stronger model's answer (H27): the generator given the detector
model's own plain answer as a hint, and the detector model used as the generator. An artefact probe (H29) reruns two
detectors on ConflictBank with the passage order reversed and with every passage paraphrased by one model.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
On RAMDocs an answer is correct if it includes every gold answer and no wrong answer \citep{wang2025madam}. Our primary
metric reads ``includes'' literally: after normalisation, each gold or wrong answer must appear as a whole-word span
of the extracted final answer. A bidirectional variant, which also counts a predicted answer contained in a gold one,
is the robustness metric. We chose the one-way metric before the confirmatory run because it is closer to MADAM-RAG's
published score (Appendix~\ref{app:repro}), and we compare conditions only within our own harness. On WikiContradict
an answer is correct if it contains both sides (bidirectional matching); on ConflictBank and RAGuard we use the RAMDocs metrics. We also report
the share of answers that contain the misinformation, the share that abstain (``unknown''), and accuracy among answered
questions. An LLM judge (gpt-6.1-terra) agrees with the primary metric on \judgeAgree\% of \judgeN\ stratified
conflict-question outputs ($\kappa=\judgeKappa$); the metric is slightly conservative, missing some paraphrases. A
200-output sheet for a human audit is released, but it has not been labelled.

\paragraph{Statistics.}
We average each question's score over seeds and report paired differences from the plain format with
bootstrap 95\% CIs that resample questions (10,000 resamples) and two-sided sign-flip permutation tests
\citep{efron1993bootstrap,dror2018hitchhiker} (10,000 permutations, extended to 200,000 when none reaches the observed
statistic). A \emph{family} is the set of tests one hypothesis registers (e.g.\ H15 across generators); a registered
test takes its family's Holm-adjusted $p$ \citep{holm1979simple}, the \nExploratory\ exploratory comparisons of the
condition tables form one family, and the hypothesis tables add a Benjamini--Hochberg $q$ over all registered tests.
Only results significant under Holm are called significant or set in bold; CIs are unadjusted and are not read as
significance. A result is \emph{robust} if it also holds under the bidirectional metric. A null difference is not read
as equivalence without a TOST \citep{lakens2017equivalence} at $\pm$3 points. Selective accuracy is a ratio (correct
over answered), tested by swapping conditions within questions. Pooled estimates use a multilevel model with random
effects for generator, benchmark and setting. Appendix~\ref{app:repro} gives the minimum detectable effect.

\paragraph{Cost.}
Token counts come from the API's usage reports. All experiments, pilots included, cost \totalSpend\ in API fees
(prices checked on 2026-10-27; Appendix~\ref{app:repro}); Qwen ran locally.

Flagging exactly the gold contradictions in the neutral format lowers gpt-4o-mini's RAMDocs conflict-question accuracy
by \adOracleFlagConf\ points \dOracleFlagConfCI\ from \plainConf\% (H2), and gpt-4.1's by \agptOracleFlagConf\ (H9).
How depends on what the prompt asks for (Table~\ref{tab:risk}).

\paragraph{Asked for every valid answer, the model adds the misinformation.} Under RAMDocs' default prompt, which asks
for every valid answer, the neutral flag raises the share of gpt-4o-mini's
answers that contain the misinformation from \zmRdMiniPlainWrong\% to \zmRdMiniOracleFlagWrong\%: told that two
passages conflict, it reports both sides. Every OpenAI and Qwen generator moves the same way; both Claude models move
the other way (Haiku on RAMDocs \zmRdHaikuPlainWrong\% to \zmRdHaikuOracleFlagWrong\%).

\paragraph{Asked for one answer, it abstains.} With a prompt stating that the question has one answer, the neutral
flag costs gpt-4o-mini \azSgMiniOracleFlagConflict\ points on ConflictBank, gpt-4.1 \azSgFourOracleFlagConflict\ and
gpt-6-luna \azSgLunaOracleFlagConflict\ (H15, H19), and changes accuracy insignificantly for gpt-6.1-terra,
Qwen2.5-7B, either Claude model (H25) and every generator on RAGuard (H26). Of gpt-4o-mini's misinformed answers, \trSgMiniNeutralMisinfoAbstain\% abstain with the flag and
\trSgMiniNeutralMisinfoCorrect\% become correct (\trNullSgMiniMisinfoCorrect\% between two unflagged seeds), and
\trSgMiniNeutralCorrectAbstain\% of its correct answers also abstain (H21). When ``unknown'' is not allowed (H20), the flag has no
significant effect for gpt-4o-mini (\hTwentyFcMiniFlag), gpt-6-luna (\hTwentyFcLunaFlag) or gpt-6.1-terra
(\hTwentyFcTerraFlag), equivalence not established, and a small one for Claude Haiku (\hTwentyFcHaikuFlag).

\paragraph{Across settings.} In a multilevel model over the \neuMlK\ settings (Table~\ref{tab:pooled}; exploratory),
fitted with the settings' sampling covariance (they share questions), the pooled effect is \neuMlMu\ points
\neuMlCI\ (\neuMlCIDesign\ conditional on these settings), and a new setting would fall in \neuMlPI. It is
\neuPolMinimal\ \neuPolMinimalCI\ under the minimal prompt, which states no answer policy, \neuPolMulti\ under

correct, and the flag raises accuracy (H8: \wikiOracleFlag).

\subsection{Naming the false side: what an oracle can deliver}
\label{sec:direction}
With the same oracle pairs, the directional flag beats the neutral and one-wrong flags in every registered comparison
but one (H3, H9--H12, H15, H16, H19, H25, H26; gpt-6.1-terra on RAGuard is not significant). On ConflictBank it gains
\azSgTerraOracleFlagDirectionalConflict\ to \azSgLunaOracleFlagDirectionalConflict\ points for the OpenAI generators
and \azSgSonnetOracleFlagDirectionalConflict\ to \azSgHaikuOracleFlagDirectionalConflict\ for Claude, and it also
helps under forced choice (H20), majority misinformation and on RAGuard (H26). On RAMDocs it gains \adOracleDirConf\ points with gpt-4o-mini and
up to \azRdTerraOracleFlagDirectionalConflict\ with gpt-6.1-terra, but not significantly for Qwen2.5-7B or for
gpt-4o-mini under the minimal prompt (H10). The ConflictBank ranges are for the one-answer prompt.

These rows are a ceiling, not a detector result. Telling the model ``the answer $X$ is false'', with no passage
marked, does as well: no registered comparison differs significantly (H23; \keyDiffMin\ to \keyDiffMax\ points), and
pooled over the \PoolTwentyThreeK\ comparisons, with a bootstrap that resamples each benchmark's questions jointly

\label{sec:real}

accuracy over all ConflictBank questions for \nRealSgPos\ of \nRealSgTests\ generators (gpt-6-luna,

\paragraph{A wrong flag costs more than a right one gains.} In a randomised test (H31), every ConflictBank conflict
question got a flag on its true contradiction, naming the false side on a random \nRandRight\ questions and the
correct side on the other \nRandWrong. A right flag raises accuracy by \hThirtyOneAMin\ to \hThirtyOneAMax\ points and
a wrong one lowers it by \ahThirtyOneBMax\ to \ahThirtyOneBMin\ (Figure~\ref{fig:reliability}, left). The net is
significantly negative for \nHThirtyOneCSig\ of \nHThirtyOneCTests\ generators at an even mix (H31c) and for
\nRealThirtyOneSig\ at the split as run (\shareRight\% right; gpt-6-luna \realThirtyOneLuna\ \realThirtyOneLunaCI).
Below a break-even direction precision $p^\star=-W/(R-W)$, for right- and wrong-flag effects $R$ and $W$, randomised
flags on true pairs do net harm: \beMini\ \beMiniCI\ for gpt-4o-mini, \beLuna\ for gpt-6-luna, \beHaiku\ for Claude
Haiku and \beTerra\ \beTerraCI\ for gpt-6.1-terra (Table~\ref{tab:breakeven}; exploratory). Right- and wrong-flag
follow rates overlap (descriptive; Table~\ref{tab:breakeven}) except gpt-6.1-terra's, so $p^\star$ rises mainly
because an accurate generator has more to lose. It is a calibration: it carries over to a real detector only if the
questions it flags are typical and it flags nothing else.

\paragraph{Real detectors are selective.} Across H24's \hTwentyFourCells\ detector--generator cells
(Table~\ref{tab:selection}), comparing precision with $p^\star$ can mislead: the gpt-4o-mini detector's precision
(\selHaikuMiniPrec\%) is just above Claude Haiku's break-even, implying \selHaikuMiniMarg\ points, but the cell's effect is
\selHaikuMiniObs: its flags on a correct passage (\selHaikuMiniWrongShare\% of questions) change accuracy there by
\selHaikuMiniWrongGain. With each cell's coverage and no-flag accuracy,
$\hat\Delta=\frac1n\sum_i[d_i(1-a_i)f_R-w_ia_if_W]$ ($d_i$: the flag names the false side; $w_i$: it marks a correct
passage; $a_i$: no-flag accuracy; $f_R,f_W$: H31's follow rates) tracks the \predEightN\ cells with an H31 generator
($r=\predEightR$ \predEightRCI, mean error \predEightMae\ points, against \predGenMae\ for each generator's mean over
its other cells), mostly through the cell-level rates (\predMargMae\ with cell means alone); it still misses the
Haiku cell (\predHaikuMini). It is a retrospective decomposition, defined after the cells were seen (with no-flag
answers from another seed, $r=\predXfR$). The registered H24 correlation is uninformative, since generator accuracy
enters both sides. The least precise detector (gpt-4o-mini, \dirPrecMini\%) lowers the accuracy of the four most
accurate generators, significantly for gpt-6.1-terra (H19).

\paragraph{What the detector contributes is its verdict.} Passing the detector's judgement as an answer key (``the
answer $X$ is false'' for the answer its suspect passage supports, no passage marked) matches the directional flag
(H30: \hThirtyAMin\ to \hThirtyAMax\ points per cell, none significant; pooled over the \PoolThirtyAK\ cells the flag
is ahead by \PoolThirtyA\ \PoolThirtyACI, equivalent within $\pm$3) and beats the detector model's own answer as a
hint by \PoolThirtyB\ \PoolThirtyBCI. That hint does worse than the flag in \nHTwentySevenAPos\ of
\nHTwentySevenATests\ cells and never better (H27).

\paragraph{When conflicts are rare.} The LLM detector also flags clean questions (\cleanFprLlm\% of RAMDocs'). At
a 5\% share of conflict questions its flag's estimate is negative in \prevNegFive\ of \prevN\ correlated settings
(bootstrap \prevNegFiveCI; significantly in \prevSigNeg, positive in \prevSigPos; Table~\ref{tab:prevalence}).

\paragraph{Genuine conflicts and baselines.} Reordering \citep{liu2024lost} or paraphrasing passages changes the
detectors' hit rate insignificantly (H29). Where both sides are valid, direction does damage (H28): on WikiContradict the LLM directional
flag lowers the share of answers giving both sides by \ahTwentyEightWcMiniDir\ to \ahTwentyEightWcLunaDir\ points
(and of answers correct under the benchmark's own rubric by \awikiOrigMiniEffPt\ to \awikiOrigLunaEffPt;
Appendix~\ref{app:semantic}), and on RAMDocs' same-name questions removal costs every generator
\ahTwentyEightAmbMiniRem\ to \ahTwentyEightAmbLunaRem\ points. Against published defences, with answer policies matched and TrustRAG's
system message restored, the LLM directional flag beats TrustRAG by \blHFourteenCbs\ points on ConflictBank and
\blHFourteenRd\ on RAMDocs, but not under majority misinformation (\blHFourteenMj); the registered H14, with
mismatched policies, shows no win (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}).
\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

A neutral flag leaves the model to its answer policy, so it suits only a one-answer policy with a measured cost for
wrong answers \citep{wen2025abstention}. A directional flag is advice that generators follow whether
right or wrong, as with sycophancy \citep{sharma2024sycophancy}; it pays only above a break-even precision, as in
human--AI complementarity \citep{bansal2021accurate,mozannar2020defer}. That break-even calibrates randomised flags on
true pairs; a selective detector needs its own test, which we did not run.
\section*{Limitations}

\paragraph{Models.} We test seven generators from three families: four OpenAI models (gpt-4o-mini, gpt-4.1 and two
current reasoning models), Claude Haiku 5.5 and Sonnet 5.5, and Qwen2.5-7B at 4-bit precision. Gemini serves only as a judge, and we run
no large open model. Qwen2.5-7B, gpt-6.1-terra and Claude Sonnet ran with one seed, as did gpt-4.1 outside RAMDocs, and the reasoning and Claude models
only at their default temperature, so their sampling is not deterministic, and Claude ran through Anthropic's
OpenAI-compatible endpoint. The crossed reliability test (H24) covers \hTwentyFourCells\ detector--generator cells on one benchmark, with six
detectors, so any test of a detector-level property has little power (an exact permutation has \hRedoNPerm\
arrangements); the registered gap turned out to be dominated by the generator's own accuracy. The follow-rate
account of the crossed cells was defined after seeing them and uses follow rates measured on the same questions, so it
is a retrospective decomposition; no outcome-blind question set tests it, and the break-even precision is a
calibration for randomised flags on true pairs, not a validated rule for selective detectors. Concurrent runs gave
a detector slightly different outputs in different cells; its output is identical across its cells for at least
\frozenShareMin\% of questions, and restricting each cell to those moves no cell's gain by more than \frozenMaxShift\
points. The LLM detector is a single
prompt. The published baselines are reimplemented (Astute RAG has no official code; restoring TrustRAG's system message
changed little), NLI sees only a sentence-sized window, and we do not compare
against a trained detector such as ConflictRAG's \citep{wang2026conflictrag}.

\paragraph{Data and power.} The confirmatory evidence comes from two benchmarks: RAMDocs (300 questions, 139 of them
conflict questions) and ConflictBank (400 sampled questions, 300 with conflicts). In both, the misinformation is
synthetic, made by editing or generating text around a substituted answer; ConflictBank's passages are LLM-written
on both sides. RAGuard's misleading side is real text, but its labels are behavioural (whether GPT-4 judged the claim
correctly with the post), and an LLM audit finds only \rgAudContradict\% of sampled supporting--misleading pairs to
contradict (Appendix~\ref{app:semantic}), so RAGuard's oracle is a label transfer, not a factual oracle. With its posts in context
generators abstain on \rgAbstMin--\rgAbstMax\% of conflict claims, which is why accuracy on this true/false task
falls below chance; on the claims they answer it is \rgAnsMin--\rgAnsMax\%, against \rgCbMin--\rgCbMax\% closed-book. RAMDocs' document labels are imperfect. We exclude 35 hold-out
questions whose status depends on documents we could not label without human annotation, and we check that the
conclusions survive treating those documents as misinformation. Our comparisons detect effects of about
\mdeMed\ points with 80\% power, so smaller effects, including small harms on clean questions, may be missed.
WikiContradict has genuine conflicts but no misinformation side. CONFLICTS shows no significant effect for any
condition; scored only on items with a reference answer, it can neither confirm nor refute our findings. The majority variant tests one case where the false side is
more numerous, but its extra wrong-side passages are generated for the same substitution, which a real attacker
would vary, and they include ConflictBank's temporal and semantic conflicts, which we count as misinformation. On
RAGuard every generator is more accurate closed-book than with the posts (gpt-4o-mini \zcRgMiniConflict\% against
\zpRgMiniConflict\%), so its posts make a weak retrieval setting. The current
reasoning models distrust ConflictBank's generated passages when asked to judge them (Appendix~\ref{app:more}),
which suggests that generated benchmarks may not measure how such models treat real documents. Reversing and
paraphrasing the passages change the detectors' hit rate insignificantly (H29), but equivalence within $\pm$5 points
is shown for only one of four changes, so position and surface style are not ruled out as sources of their direction,
nor are deeper regularities of generated text.

\paragraph{Metric.} RAMDocs ships no scorer. Our primary metric reconstructs the published definition and scores our
MADAM-RAG port close to the published number on the pilot split, but the published subset is unknown, so this is not a
reproduction. The metric is string matching: it misses paraphrases, and partial answers interact with ``no wrong
answer''. An outside-family LLM judge agrees with it on \semAgree\% of \semN\ paired outputs; the matcher's misses
depend on the condition (more under answer keys and directional flags) but move no contrast by more than \semDidMax\
points (Appendix~\ref{app:semantic}). That sample cannot confirm the $\pm$3-point equivalence claims, and no human
labels check either scorer: the paired, blinded audit sheet we release is unlabelled. We report a more lenient variant throughout and mark any conclusion that depends on the choice.

\paragraph{Direction and the grid.} Oracle direction is not distinguishable from an explicit answer key (H23), so the
oracle rows are ceilings, not findings about detectors. In the synthetic grid the gain rises with direction accuracy, but the grid cannot separate adjacent levels. The gpt-4o-mini LLM
detector's direction precision (of its flags on a true contradiction, the share naming the false side; distinct from
its pair precision, the share of its flagged pairs that are true contradictions) is \dirAccLlm\% on RAMDocs and
\cbDirAccLlm\% on ConflictBank; its hit rate per question is lower, because it also misses pairs. Its
directional flag's gain over all ConflictBank questions with gpt-4o-mini is not significant after correction (H15),
and with stronger generators it hurts. Synthetic false flags are spread uniformly and their suspect side is random, whereas
real detectors concentrate their errors. The grid predicts real detectors only roughly, and three detectors cannot
show whether document- or pair-level summaries predict better.

\paragraph{Pre-registration.} The original hypotheses (H1--H9) were registered before the confirmatory run. H10--H31
were registered in eight addenda, after the earlier results were known though before their own runs, and the only
timestamp is the repository's commit history, not a third-party registry. Holm controls error within each family, not
across the programme: of \fdrN\ registered tests, \fdrHolm\ are significant under Holm and \fdrBH\ under a
Benjamini--Hochberg FDR over all of them (both shown in the hypothesis tables). Several analyses were chosen after seeing
data and are labelled as such: the TOST margin, the single- and multi-answer split, the document-level placement
axes, the post-hoc comparison of detector and
generator reliability that H24 later tested, H24's mechanism score, and the refinement of permutation $p$-values at
their floor.

\paragraph{Scope.} We study flags passed to the generator as text. Learned interfaces such as soft prompts or
credibility fine-tuning \citep{ye2026tcr,pan2024credibility}, and retrieval-side filtering, may follow different
trade-offs. We test one adversarial case, a detector that always names the wrong side, but not an attacker who controls
which pairs are flagged.

\section*{Ethics Statement}
This work uses public benchmarks and commercial model APIs, and involves no human subjects or personal data. Its main
risk is dual use. Our results show that a false ``likely misinformation'' label, or the removal of a passage, can
change a RAG system's answers: an inverted oracle that names the correct side as misinformation costs \aadvDirConf\
points on RAMDocs and \azCbMiniOracleInvFlagDirectionalConflict\ on ConflictBank as a flag, and \aadvRemConf\ on RAMDocs
when the passage it names is removed. An adversary who can trigger a deployed detector could exploit this
\citep{zou2025poisonedrag}. We therefore report harm on conflict-free questions alongside every gain, and we argue that
systems should measure what a detector does at the document level before acting on its flags.

\paragraph{The heuristic detector.}
CAAG scores a pair only if its MiniLM \citep{reimers2019sbert} cosine similarity reaches $\theta_s=0.65$. Its score
is the sum of a negation and contrast asymmetry term, a named-entity mismatch term (differing dates, numbers, money or
percentages) and a temporal-marker term, multiplied by the similarity. A pair is flagged if the score reaches
$\theta_c=0.35$. Its per-pair flag rate on the confirmatory split ($6.22\%$) sets the rate of the random detector.
CAAG is the detector we set out to study. As a neutral flag it did not beat random flags of the same volume, and
as a removal rule it only deleted correct passages (\dCaagRemClean\ points on clean questions, \dCaagRemConf\ on
conflict ones). Its false flags fall mostly on pairs that should both be reported: 134 of its 221 false flags on the
hold-out are same-name ambiguity pairs, which its entity-mismatch signal flags because they mention different dates or
numbers. We read this as a warning about lexical contradiction cues in general \citep{gururangan2018annotation},
rather than as a problem with a particular threshold. Retuning illustrates why F1 misleads. Over a 121-setting sweep, CAAG's
best pair F1 is \caagBestFOne, above NLI's, at $\theta_s=\caagBestThS$ and $\theta_c=\caagBestThC$. But it gets
there by flagging \caagBestRate\% of all pairs and \caagBestCleanFpr\% of clean questions, which our grid predicts
would be harmful under removal (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}).

\paragraph{Original CAAG format.}
Rendering CAAG's flags in its original format, with typed lines and confidence scores (``factual conflict (confidence:
0.41)''), changes accuracy by \origCaagAll\ points overall (\origCaagConf\ on conflict questions, \origCaagClean\ on
clean ones). This is no different from the uniform format.

\paragraph{Pilot study (exploratory).}
On the 200-question pilot split, scored with the bidirectional metric, the same pattern appeared before any
confirmatory run. Oracle neutral flags changed conflict-question accuracy by $-$10.5 points, directional flags by
$+$11.8, and removing the suspect by $+$28.4. The pilots also showed that the original \textsc{remove} strategy,
which deletes an arbitrary side of each pair, removes correct documents. They are why we added directional formats,
synthetic direction accuracy and document-level placement. All pilot runs are released.

\paragraph{Phase-0 audit of CAAG on NarrativeQA.}
CAAG was designed for long-document QA. On 100 NarrativeQA \citep{kocisky2018narrativeqa} questions from 50 stories
(BM25 top-10 chunks from a single coherent story), it flags at least one pair in 54\% of questions, 166 pairs in all.
A single story rarely contradicts itself, so most of these flags are likely false; the flagged pairs are released for
annotation. This is the setting where flags are most likely to be false.

\subsection{Where the directional flag fails (exploratory)}
\label{app:single}
After seeing the minimal-skeleton run, we split conflict questions by the number of gold answers. \nConfMulti\ of
\the\numexpr\nConfMulti+\nConfSingle\relax\ have several (same-name ambiguity \emph{and} misinformation), and a strict
answer must list all of them. On the \nConfSingle\ single-answer conflict questions, the directional flag's estimate
is positive in every setting, from \yQwenSingleOracleDir\ points (Qwen) to \yMiniSingleOracleDir\ (gpt-4o-mini), both
with the default skeleton. On multi-answer questions it changes little except with gpt-4.1 (gpt-4o-mini
\yMiniMultiOracleDir, and \yMiniMinMultiOracleDir\ with the minimal skeleton; gpt-4.1 \yFourMultiOracleDir). One
reading is that a warning about one passage makes the smaller models more conservative about the legitimate
alternative answers too; we did not test this. Removal does not depend on the model acting on a warning, and it gains
in both groups.

\subsection{Real detectors}
CAAG flags \cleanFprCaag\% of clean questions. Its neutral flags do not beat random flags at the same rate (H5;
\dCaagFlagAll\ vs.\ \dRandFlagAll\ points overall). The 90\% CI of the difference, [\tostLo, \tostHi], rules out a

\paragraph{Construction.}
Each synthetic detector (Section~\ref{sec:synthetic}) is built from the gold labels. Each gold contradiction pair is
flagged with probability $r$. False flags are drawn from non-contradicting pairs at a single per-pair rate, set over
the whole split so that expected precision is $p$. That rate is dataset-wide, so conflict-free questions receive
false flags too. A true flag names the gold misinformation side with probability $d$, and otherwise the other side.
A false flag has no misinformation side, so its suspect is picked uniformly. Each pair gets one uniform draw for
flagging and one for direction, and every cell reuses them. Cells are therefore nested: raising $r$ or $d$, or
lowering $p$, only adds flags or corrections. This keeps comparisons across cells paired.

\paragraph{Results.}
Figure~\ref{fig:grid} replaces the oracle with synthetic detectors of known quality. Gains rise with direction
accuracy in both formats. Only the cells with perfect direction are significant on all questions (directional flag
\gDirOneDOneAll, removal \gRemOneDOneAll\ points at perfect precision), but neighbouring cells at direction accuracy
0.75 have similar point estimates on conflict questions, so this is a dose-response, not a threshold: with 36 cells
under Holm, the grid has little power to separate adjacent levels. Precision matters mainly for removal. On clean questions a cell's prompt does not depend
on direction accuracy, so the false-flag cells reduce to \hFourRemN\ distinct comparisons per format. Removal is
significantly harmful in \hFourRemHarm\ of them, the directional flag in \hFourDirHarm\ (H4a): false flags make the
model delete correct passages, which costs clean questions \agRemHalfDOneClean\ points at precision 0.5 and
\agRemQuDOneClean\ at 0.25, while synthetic false directional flags cost at most two points. The harm tracks the
share of clean questions that lose a correct document (Spearman $\rho=\hFourRho$ over \hFourRhoCells\ distinct
cells, 95\% CI \hFourRhoCI\ from a question-level bootstrap; H4b). Removal hurt clean questions significantly more
than the directional flag in \hFourRemWorse\ of \hFourMatched\ matched cells (H4c; we set no threshold, so we report
the count). Removal is the more powerful and the more fragile interface: its best cell gains twice the directional
flag's on all questions, but below precision 0.75 it is the only format that does net harm.


\paragraph{Placing real detectors on the grid.}
\label{sec:placement}
Can the grid predict a real detector's effect from a few summary numbers? We compare two ways of summarising a
detector. \emph{Pair-level}: its pair precision, recall and direction accuracy. \emph{Document-level}: what it does
to each subset's documents in a given format. For conflict questions, that is the share whose misinformation documents
are all marked and the share that have a correct document marked; for clean questions, the share that have a correct
document marked. Here ``marked'' means removed (Remove) or headed as misinformation (Directional flag). The prediction
for a subset is the inverse-distance-weighted mean effect of the four nearest grid cells in that space.

\paragraph{Does the grid predict real detectors?}

If the grid only described synthetic detectors, it would be of limited use. Figure~\ref{fig:placement} (Appendix~\ref{app:extra}) tests
whether it predicts real ones (H6). As pre-registered, we bootstrap the whole prediction (cell effects, every detector's
location and the interpolation) over questions, and ask whether the actual effect falls inside the 95\% interval.
Locating detectors by what they do to documents (the share of conflict questions whose misinformation is marked, and
the share of questions that have a correct document marked) puts \hSixDocIn\ of \hSixN\ effects inside; locating them
by pair precision, recall and direction accuracy puts \hSixPairIn\ inside. Document-level errors are smaller on
average (\hSixDocMae\ vs.\ \hSixPairMae\ points), but the paired difference, \hSixMaeDiff\ \hSixMaeDiffCI\ (exact
sign-flip $p=\hSixMaeP$), is not significant over \hSixN\ cases. Both spaces miss the same case, the LLM detector's
removal gain on conflict questions: one correctly directed pair is enough to remove a misinformation document, so the
detector removes the misinformation on \misGoneLlm\% of conflict questions despite its low pair recall. We chose the
document-level axes after seeing the LLM detector on the pilot split. The grid is a useful first estimate of a
detector's effect, but with three detectors we cannot claim that one summary predicts better than the other.
