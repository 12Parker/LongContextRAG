\section{Introduction}

Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) \citep{lewis2020rag} hands a language model passages that may disagree. Some
disagreements are genuine: two sources report different findings, or two people share a name. Others are planted or
stale misinformation \citep{zou2025poisonedrag,wang2025madam}. A natural fix is to detect the contradiction and tell
the generator. Recent systems pair contradiction detectors with prompts that flag conflicting passages
\citep{wang2026conflictrag}, and prompting a model about conflicts can help \citep{cattan2025conflicts}. These systems
report detection F1 and end-to-end accuracy, but not how the two connect. What should the flag say, and what must the
detector get right for it to help?

We fix the retrieved passages and vary, independently, the \emph{detector} (heuristic, NLI, LLM, random placebo,
oracle, and synthetic detectors of chosen quality) and the \emph{format} that passes its output to the generator: a
neutral ``these passages may conflict'' flag, a flag that says one side is false without saying which, a
\emph{directional} flag that names the likely misinformation, or removal of the suspect passage. After pilots on
RAMDocs \citep{wang2025madam}, we pre-registered our hypotheses (H1--H9) and tested them on 300 held-out questions;
eight addenda (H10--H31), each committed before its runs, extended the study to ConflictBank \citep{su2024conflictbank}
(including a variant where the misinformation outnumbers the truth), to RAGuard \citep{zeng2025raguard}, whose
misleading side is real Reddit posts, and to seven generators from OpenAI, Anthropic and Qwen. Controls separate what a
flag conveys: a forced-choice prompt with no ``unknown'', an answer key that names the wrong answer without pointing at
any passage, flags whose direction is randomised, the detector's verdict and the detector model's own answer given as
hints, and a crossed design in which six detectors serve six generators. WikiContradict \citep{hou2024wikicontradict}, whose conflicts are genuine, is the contrast case.

Our findings:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item \textbf{A correct neutral flag's effect depends on the answer policy.} Under a prompt that asks for every
valid answer, models add the misinformation to their answer; under a one-answer prompt that allows ``unknown'', they
abstain, on misinformed and correct answers alike; under forced choice, the flag has little effect. Whether the
abstention is worth it depends on the cost of a wrong answer (exploratory).
\item \textbf{An oracle's direction is a ceiling.} Naming the false side helps on every benchmark that labels one,
but an answer key that names the wrong answer does nearly as well (H23).
\item \textbf{With a real detector, its wrong flags decide.} With a gpt-4o-mini detector, \nRealSgPos\ of
\nRealSgTests\ generators gain significantly on ConflictBank and \nRealSgNeg\ loses. When direction is randomised
(H31), generators follow right and wrong flags at similar rates, so a wrong flag costs an accurate generator more than
a right one gains. Across \hTwentyFourCells\ crossed detector--generator cells, the registered reliability gap tracks
the gain mostly through the generator's own accuracy; the detector's verdict as an answer key does as well as the
flag (H30), and the detector model's own answer as a hint does not (H27).
\item \textbf{Direction is unsafe on genuine conflicts.} On WikiContradict the LLM directional flag, and removal, cut
the share of answers that give both valid sides (H28).
\end{itemize}
These results suggest, though we do not test end to end, measuring a detector's wrong-side rate on questions the
target generator already answers correctly, letting the detector abstain, passing direction only where conflicts are
likely to be misinformation, and choosing a neutral flag only together with an answer policy and a cost for wrong
answers. We
release the harness, pre-registrations and every run.\footnote{Code, the pre-registration and all run outputs are in
the anonymised supplementary material and will be released publicly on acceptance.}
